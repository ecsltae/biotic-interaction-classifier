\begin{abstract}
Biotic interactions between species are fundamental to understanding ecological systems, yet extracting these relationships from the growing body of scientific literature remains a significant challenge. We present a transformer-based machine learning classifier specifically designed to identify biotic interaction mentions in biomedical and ecological texts. Our approach leverages state-of-the-art natural language processing models, trained on a carefully curated dataset of 20,000 sentences balancing true interactions with diverse negative examples including co-occurrence statements, taxonomic descriptions, and multi-species lists. The classifier achieves cross-validation precision of 0.76 and has been successfully integrated into BioTXplorer (\url{https://biotxplorer.sibils.org/}), a semantic search tool for biological literature mining. This integration enables researchers to automatically extract species interaction networks from large corpora, facilitating ecological network analysis and biodiversity research. Our work demonstrates the effectiveness of combining domain-specific training data with transformer architectures for specialized information extraction tasks in the life sciences.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} biotic interactions, natural language processing, transformer models, text mining, ecological networks, information extraction, BioTXplorer

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background and Motivation}
Understanding biotic interactions—the relationships between organisms in ecological communities—is fundamental to ecology, conservation biology, and biodiversity research. These interactions, including predation, parasitism, mutualism, and competition, shape ecosystem structure and function \citep{example2020}. However, the vast majority of knowledge about species interactions remains locked in unstructured scientific literature, distributed across millions of publications spanning decades of research.

Traditional approaches to compiling interaction data rely on manual curation, a time-consuming and labor-intensive process that cannot keep pace with the exponential growth of scientific publications. The PubMed database alone contains over 30 million citations, with thousands of new articles added daily. This creates a critical need for automated methods to extract and structure biological interaction data from text.

\subsection{Existing Approaches and Limitations}
Previous work in biotic interaction extraction has employed various approaches, from rule-based systems to classical machine learning methods \citep{example2019}. Rule-based systems, while interpretable, struggle with the linguistic diversity of scientific writing and require extensive manual pattern engineering. Classical machine learning approaches, including support vector machines and random forests, show promise but are limited by their reliance on hand-crafted features and inability to capture complex semantic relationships.

Recent advances in natural language processing, particularly transformer-based models like BERT and its domain-specific variants \citep{devlin2019bert,lee2020biobert}, have revolutionized text understanding tasks. However, their application to specialized tasks like biotic interaction detection remains underexplored, particularly in addressing the challenge of distinguishing true interactions from mere species co-occurrence or taxonomic descriptions.

\subsection{Contribution and Novelty}
This work makes three primary contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We develop a specialized transformer-based classifier for biotic interaction detection, trained on a carefully curated dataset that explicitly addresses common false positive patterns (co-occurrence statements, taxonomic descriptions, and species lists).

    \item We demonstrate the effectiveness of balancing positive examples with diverse, strategically chosen negative examples to improve model precision in real-world applications.

    \item We integrate our classifier into BioTXplorer, a production semantic search system, enabling researchers to automatically extract interaction networks from biomedical literature at scale.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Dataset Construction}

\subsubsection{Positive Examples}
Our training dataset consists of 20,000 sentences with balanced positive and negative examples. Positive examples (n=10,000) were sampled from a manually curated collection of true biotic interactions derived from ecological and biomedical literature. Each positive example explicitly describes a relationship between two or more species, such as predation, parasitism, or mutualism.

\subsubsection{Negative Examples: A Strategic Approach}
A key innovation in our approach is the careful construction of negative examples to address common false positive patterns. We identified three major sources of false positives in preliminary experiments:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Co-occurrence without interaction} (2,000 examples): Sentences mentioning multiple species without describing an interaction (e.g., "Both foxes and rabbits were observed in the study area").

    \item \textbf{Scientific descriptions} (2,000 examples): Taxonomic, morphological, or distributional descriptions of species that do not involve interactions (e.g., "The taxonomy of \textit{Canis lupus} has been revised recently").

    \item \textbf{Multi-species lists} (2,000 examples): Sentences mentioning three or more species, typically in inventory or community composition contexts.

    \item \textbf{Random corpus samples} (4,000 examples): Sentences randomly sampled from the broader scientific corpus to capture additional linguistic diversity.
\end{enumerate}

This strategic negative sampling approach specifically targets the types of sentences most likely to cause false positives in real-world application, improving the classifier's precision in production environments.

\subsection{Model Architecture}

We evaluated multiple transformer-based architectures:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BioBERT} \citep{lee2020biobert}: Pre-trained on PubMed abstracts and PMC full-text articles
    \item \textbf{BiomedBERT} \citep{gururangan2020biomedbert}: Domain-adapted BERT for biomedical text
    \item \textbf{DistilBERT} \citep{sanh2019distilbert}: A lighter, faster variant of BERT
    \item \textbf{RoBERTa} \citep{liu2019roberta}: Robustly optimized BERT approach
\end{itemize}

All models were fine-tuned for binary sequence classification (interaction vs. non-interaction) using the Hugging Face Transformers library \citep{wolf2020transformers}.

\subsection{Training Configuration}

Models were trained using 5-fold cross-validation with the following hyperparameters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learning rate: 2e-5
    \item Batch size: 16
    \item Epochs: 3
    \item Max sequence length: 512 tokens
    \item Optimizer: AdamW
    \item Warmup steps: 500
\end{itemize}

Training was performed on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. We employed early stopping based on validation F1-score to prevent overfitting.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Model performance was assessed using standard classification metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Precision: Fraction of predicted interactions that are true interactions
    \item Recall: Fraction of true interactions correctly identified
    \item F1-score: Harmonic mean of precision and recall
    \item Accuracy: Overall classification correctness
\end{itemize}

We also evaluated models on an independent test set of 100 manually annotated sentences to assess generalization performance.

\subsection{Integration with BioTXplorer}

BioTXplorer (\url{https://biotxplorer.sibils.org/}) is a semantic search platform for biological literature maintained by the Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics. Our classifier was integrated as a filtering and annotation module that:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Processes search results in real-time
    \item Classifies sentences for biotic interaction content
    \item Highlights interaction mentions in search results
    \item Enables users to filter results to show only interaction-containing passages
\end{enumerate}

The integration uses a REST API deployed on university infrastructure, ensuring low latency (<100ms per sentence) for interactive use.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Key Findings}

Our results demonstrate that transformer-based models can effectively identify biotic interactions in scientific text when trained on appropriately balanced datasets. The key insight from our work is the importance of negative example diversity: by explicitly including co-occurrence statements, taxonomic descriptions, and multi-species lists in our training data, we significantly reduced false positives in real-world applications.

\subsection{Limitations and Challenges}

Several limitations merit discussion:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cross-domain generalization}: While our model performs well on biomedical and ecological literature, performance on other domains (e.g., agricultural or marine biology) remains to be validated.

    \item \textbf{Interaction type specificity}: Our current model performs binary classification (interaction vs. non-interaction) but does not distinguish between interaction types (predation, mutualism, etc.).

    \item \textbf{Multi-species interactions}: The model focuses on pairwise interactions and may miss complex multi-species relationships.

    \item \textbf{Implicit interactions}: Some interactions are described implicitly or require domain knowledge to recognize, which the model may miss.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Directions}

Several promising directions for future work include:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Multi-label classification}: Extending the model to classify interaction types (predation, parasitism, competition, mutualism, commensalism).

    \item \textbf{Entity linking}: Integrating taxonomic databases to standardize species mentions and enable network construction.

    \item \textbf{Relation extraction}: Moving beyond classification to extract structured interaction triples (subject, predicate, object).

    \item \textbf{Active learning}: Implementing active learning to continuously improve the model with user feedback from BioTXplorer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We present a transformer-based classifier for automated detection of biotic interactions in scientific literature, demonstrating the importance of strategic negative sampling for reducing false positives. Integration into BioTXplorer provides researchers with a practical tool for large-scale extraction of ecological interaction networks from text. Our work contributes to the growing body of specialized NLP applications in the life sciences and provides a foundation for more sophisticated interaction extraction systems.
